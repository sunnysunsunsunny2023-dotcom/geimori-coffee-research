# Flat vs. Conical Burr Grinders: What the Design Differences Do and Do Not Tell You About Flavor

## Key Takeaways

- Burr **shape** describes layout; burr **design** and grinder engineering determine performance. Neither geometry is automatically "better" or limited to one brew method.
- Conical burrs are often associated with a rounder, more integrated cup; some flat burr grinders are chosen for clearer note separation. These are **starting tendencies**, not guarantees.
- Flat burrs do **not** automatically produce a more uniform grind. Particle-size distribution depends on tooth geometry, alignment, grind setting, beans, feed, RPM, and wear.
- A 2024 *Scientific Reports* study found that a higher proportion of fines can reduce coffee-bed permeability and slow espresso flow — evidence that particle distribution matters, not proof that one burr shape is universally superior.
- Compare complete grinders and their burr sets, not abstract shapes. Roast, recipe, water, and workflow change the cup as much as burr geometry.

## 1. Introduction

Conical and flat burr grinders break coffee beans in different ways. But neither burr shape automatically makes better coffee. What you taste depends on the complete burr design, the particle-size distribution it produces, the grind setting, and your brewing recipe.

This technical reference explains the mechanical differences between the two geometries, what the science can — and cannot — tell us about flavor, and how to choose a grinder based on what you actually drink. It is based on [Geimori's original guide](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/how-to-choose-the-right-coffee-grinder-conical-burrs-vs-flat-burrs-what-s-the-difference-in-flavor) and the published research it cites.

## 2. How the Two Geometries Work

### 2.1 Conical burrs

A conical burr set places a cone-shaped inner burr inside a ring-shaped outer burr. Beans enter from above, travel downward through cutting zones, and leave as ground coffee. Tooth pattern, cutting geometry, feed rate, and alignment all influence the result.

### 2.2 Flat burrs

A flat burr set uses two facing, disc-shaped burrs. Coffee enters near the center and generally travels outward through the cutting surfaces before exiting. As with conical burrs, the cutting teeth and the rest of the grinder matter more than the shape label.

> **Key distinction:** Burr shape describes the layout; burr design and grinder engineering determine how that layout performs. Neither geometry is limited to espresso or pour-over.

## 3. Particle-Size Distribution and the Evidence on Fines

Every grinder produces a distribution of particle sizes, including smaller particles commonly called *fines*. That distribution depends on burr tooth geometry, alignment, grind setting, bean properties, feed behavior, RPM, and wear — not on shape alone.

### 3.1 What the research shows

A 2024 study published in *Scientific Reports* ([doi link](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-55831-x)) found that increasing the proportion of fines could reduce coffee-bed permeability, slow flow, and increase extraction time under the conditions tested.

**Important caveats:**

- The study **did not directly compare flat and conical burr geometries.**
- It **did not test Geimori grinders.**
- It is evidence that particle-size distribution matters — not proof that one burr shape is more uniform or flavorful.

### 3.2 Flavor tendencies (not guarantees)

The comparison below is a tasting hypothesis — useful for deciding what to explore first, but not a prediction of how a particular grinder will taste:

| Cup characteristic | Conical-burr starting tendency | Flat-burr starting tendency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flavor separation | Notes may feel more blended or integrated | Individual notes may feel easier to identify |
| Body and texture | Often associated with a rounder, fuller impression | May emphasize a more defined or lighter texture |
| Espresso clarity | Can be clear, but may favor a unified presentation | Often explored when clarity is the goal |
| Sweetness | May feel broad and cohesive | May feel more distinct alongside acidity or fruit notes |
| Milk drinks | A rounded espresso can remain cohesive in milk | Distinct notes may stand out in smaller milk drinks |
| Pour-over | Can make balanced, approachable filter coffee | Often explored for separation and an open cup |

Neither column means "always." Some flat burrs are designed for full-bodied espresso, and well-engineered conical burrs can produce articulate filter brews.

## 4. Burr Shape vs. Other Grinder Variables

Burr shape does not exist in isolation. RPM, heat, retention, and static are all affected by the complete grinder design:

- **RPM and heat:** Conical does not automatically mean low-speed; flat does not automatically mean high-speed. See [Low RPM vs High RPM Grinding](brewing-science/low-rpm-vs-high-rpm-grinding.md).
- **Retention:** Grounds may remain in the burr chamber or exit chute of either type. Chute design, bellows, cleaning access, and dosing matter more than burr category. See [Coffee Grinder Retention](grinder-retention/coffee-grinder-retention-explained.md).
- **Static:** Static is affected by coffee, environment, grinding conditions, and any anti-static system — not by burr shape. See [Plasma Anti-Static Technology](anti-static-technology/plasma-anti-static-technology.md).

## 5. A Real-World Example: Geimori GU63 vs. GU64 Gen2

The Geimori lineup illustrates two ways to build a capable single-dose grinder. This is a comparison of **two complete products**, not a controlled experiment isolating burr shape — the grinders also differ in motor speed, burr options, and workflow.

| | Geimori GU63 | Geimori GU64 Gen2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burr platform | 63 mm, 8-core conical burrs | 64 mm flat burrs |
| Speed | Fixed 80 RPM | Adjustable 800–1200 RPM |
| Adjustment | Stepless, marked 0–85 scale | Stepless |
| Burr options | Conical setup with tool-free cleaning access | Multi-purpose or Filter flat burr options; quick-swap |
| Static management | Plasma anti-static | Plasma anti-static |
| Workflow emphasis | Fewer variables, straightforward daily dosing | More room to experiment with RPM and burr selection |

**GU63** may be a better first look if you want a simple fixed-speed workflow and an integrated espresso presentation. **GU64 Gen2** may appeal if you want a flat-burr platform, adjustable speed, and alternative burr sets.

Neither model guarantees a particular tasting note. Bean, roast, burr alignment, and brewing recipe still determine the result.

## 6. How to Choose: Three Questions

1. **What do I drink most days?** Start with your regular brew, not the method you try twice a year.
2. **Do I prefer a rounded, integrated cup or more obvious note separation?** Both are valid preferences, not a ranking.
3. **Do I want to adjust several variables or keep the routine simple?** Variable RPM and alternative burr sets add experimentation; fixed speed keeps daily brewing straightforward.
