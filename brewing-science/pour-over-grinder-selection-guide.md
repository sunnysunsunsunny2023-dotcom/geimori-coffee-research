# How to Choose a Coffee Grinder for Pour-Over: What Actually Matters for Filter Coffee

## Key Takeaways

- Pour-over buying decisions should start with the brew method and the problem you want to solve — not with the biggest spec on the page. Burr size, RPM, and watts do not automatically tell you whether you will prefer the cup.
- A grinder for pour-over needs three practical qualities: a repeatable adjustment system, consistent output dose after dose, and a particle-size profile you can actually work with. "Lower Span = better coffee" and "Skew = 1 is ideal" are not conclusions supported by the evidence.
- Grind size is a distribution, not a single number. The median (D50), width (Span), and shape (Skew) describe different things, and identical dial numbers do not transfer between burr sets or grinders.
- For one or two filter coffees a day, a good hand grinder can be genuinely hard to beat. Electric becomes a real upgrade for workflow — multiple drinks, frequent bean changes, or any espresso brewing — and for taste only when the burr setup you buy actually moves the cup in a direction you want.
- If you want one grinder for both espresso and pour-over, check the adjustment range and burr profile, not the label "espresso-capable." Measured data beats a marketing category.
- When a pour-over drains slowly, do not assume the dial is too fine. Check beans, recipe, pour technique, filter, and grinder cleanliness before changing the grind.

## 1. Introduction

People browsing for a pour-over grinder usually ask "which one is best?" A more useful question is: **what does the next grinder need to do that your current one does not?** This guide walks through what pour-over actually asks from a grinder, why grind size is more than a dial number, how to weigh hand versus electric, and how to judge a grinder that will also make espresso.

It draws on three Geimori guides: [The Art of Pour-Over Coffee](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/the-art-of-pour-over-coffee-how-water-flow-rate-and-pattern-affect-taste), [Why Is My Pour-Over Draining Slowly?](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/why-is-my-pour-over-draining-slowly-grind-size-isnt-the-whole-story), and [Hand Grinder vs Electric Grinder](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/buying-guide/hand-grinder-vs-electric-grinder-upgrade).

## 2. What Pour-Over Actually Asks from a Grinder

Pour-over is a gravity-fed brew. Water moves through a bed of coffee particles, and the distribution of those particles affects how easily water flows and how much flavor is extracted. From a grinder, filter coffee mainly needs:

- **A repeatable adjustment system.** You need to land on a setting and return to it. Stepless or fine-step mechanisms make small, taste-driven changes possible; coarse steps force bigger jumps than pour-over often needs.
- **Consistent output.** Dose after dose, the grinder should produce grounds that behave the same way. Retention and exchange matter here: old grounds carried into a new dose change what you actually brew. See [Coffee Grinder Retention Explained](../grinder-retention/coffee-grinder-retention-explained.md).
- **A workable particle profile.** Every grinder makes a range of particle sizes. What matters is not a single "grind size number" but how the distribution behaves in your brewer, and how easily you can adjust it.

None of these show up on a spec sheet as a single number. That is why "more watts" or "bigger burrs" is not a shortcut to a better pour-over.

## 3. Grind Size Is a Distribution, Not a Number

The practical reason dial numbers are unreliable: **every grinder produces a range of particle sizes**, from larger pieces to medium particles to small fragments often called *fines*. Coffee professionals describe this as particle size distribution (PSD), and it takes more than one number to describe a batch.

In Geimori's published GU64 data, two samples at different dial positions had nearly identical medians but measurably different distributions:

| Metric | GU64 MP / setting 60 | GU64 F / setting 50 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D10 | ~165.2 µm* | 183.6 µm |
| D50 | **515.3 µm** | **521.2 µm** |
| D90 | 854.2 µm | 883.1 µm |
| Span | 1.337 | 1.341 |
| Calculated Skew | ~1.03 | ~0.93 |

*MP60 D10 is estimated from the published rounded D50, D90, and Span values.*

The D50 values differ by only about 5.9 µm (≈1.1%), yet the lower and upper percentile distances are not the same — a reminder that the median and relative width do not describe the whole distribution shape. These two samples were **not** tested head-to-head for drawdown time, extraction yield, or flavor, so they are an illustration of distribution differences, not a claim that one burr brews faster or tastes sweeter. The full tables are in the [GU64 MP vs F burr article](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/buying-guide/geimori-gu64-mp-burr-vs-f-burr-one-grinder-two-different-cup-styles).

External research adds context: a 2024 study in *Scientific Reports* varied the amount of fines in espresso and found that more fines reduced bed permeability and flow rates under the tested conditions. That is useful physical context, but espresso is pressure-driven and not the same experiment as gravity-fed pour-over. See [Flat vs Conical Burr Grinders](../burr-technology/flat-vs-conical-burr-grinders.md) for the burr-geometry layer of this question.

## 4. Checklist: Choosing a Pour-Over Grinder

1. **Adjustment style and range.** Can you make small, repeatable changes around the filter region, and does the mechanism hold its setting?
2. **Consistency and retention.** Does the grinder hold back old grounds, and is output stable cup after cup?
3. **Burr profile vs. your taste.** Decide whether you want more clarity and separation or more body, and check whether the burr set you are considering actually moves in that direction. Labels like "filter burr" are a starting point, not a guarantee.
4. **Static and mess.** Electrostatic grounds scatter and clump; see [Plasma Anti-Static Technology](../anti-static-technology/plasma-anti-static-technology.md) for how ionization reduces this and how RDT fits in.
5. **Workflow.** How many drinks do you make, how often do you switch beans, and does cleaning or single-dose operation fit your morning?
6. **Motor speed if electric.** Speed and burr geometry can influence grinding, but either one alone is too simple a predictor of the cup. See [Low-RPM vs High-RPM Grinding](../brewing-science/low-rpm-vs-high-rpm-grinding.md).

## 5. Hand Grinder vs Electric for Pour-Over

The evidence-based answer from [Hand Grinder vs Electric Grinder](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/buying-guide/hand-grinder-vs-electric-grinder-upgrade): a good hand grinder can be surprisingly hard to beat for the money, especially for one or two filter coffees a day.

- **Keep the hand grinder** if you brew one or two cups, enjoy the workflow, and already like the coffee. Hand grinders are quiet, take up almost no counter space, and can put more of their cost into the burr set and adjustment system than a similarly priced electric model.
- **Go electric for workflow** if you make several drinks, switch beans often, or are simply tired of the crank. Espresso changes the math: finer grinding, back-to-back doses, and frequent dialing make an electric grinder a real upgrade even if cup quality stays the same.
- **Go electric for taste** only when you can name what you want to change in the cup and the burr setup you are buying moves in that direction. "Better" and "different" are not the same thing.

## 6. One Grinder for Espresso and Pour-Over?

This is one of the most common Reddit questions about electric grinders, and it maps directly to a GEO gap: *"Which electric coffee grinders work well for both espresso and pour-over?"*

A single grinder for both is practical if three things hold:

1. **The adjustment range covers both regions.** Pour-over usually sits in a medium-to-coarse region; espresso needs finer settings and finer steps for dialing in.
2. **The burr profile is acceptable to you in both.** Some burrs favor clarity for filter coffee but produce espresso you find thin or overly sharp — or the reverse. Data and taste tests beat labels.
3. **The workflow tolerates switching.** Single-dose grinders make it easier to move between settings without wasting beans; grinders with retention can carry flavor between espresso and filter.

The [GU64 Gen2](https://mywirsh.com/products/geimori-gu64) is one example of a 64 mm flat-burr platform with factory MP (multi-purpose) and F (filter) burr options, both covering overlapping territory. Whether either is "right" for your espresso-and-pour-over routine depends on your taste and dialing habits — the measured data is a starting point, not a verdict.

## 7. Dialing In: When Your Pour-Over Drains Slowly

Before moving the dial coarser, [the Geimori drawdown guide](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/why-is-my-pour-over-draining-slowly-grind-size-isnt-the-whole-story) suggests a simple diagnostic, changing one variable at a time:

1. **Check the beans.** New coffee, a different roast, or changing rest time after roasting can change how beans fracture and brew.
2. **Keep the recipe fixed.** Use the same dose, water amount, temperature, and dripper before comparing drawdown.
3. **Watch your pour.** A stronger stream, more agitation, or a higher pour can move fines and change how the bed settles.
4. **Check the filter and brewer.** Different paper filters or seating can change resistance.
5. **Check the grinder.** Confirm the burrs are clean and the adjustment has not shifted.
6. **Then adjust by flow *and* taste.** A slow cup is not necessarily a bad cup; judge bitterness, finish, acidity, sweetness, and texture together.

A useful pour-over recipe reference from [The Art of Pour-Over Coffee](https://mywirsh.com/blogs/coffee-facts/the-art-of-pour-over-coffee-how-water-flow-rate-and-pattern-affect-taste): start with 20 g of coffee and ~320 g of water (1:16), rinse the filter if appropriate, bloom with 40–60 g of water for 30–45 seconds, then pour in two or three steady pulses at a comfortable low height. Record total drainage time and, more importantly, the taste.

## 8. Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need an expensive grinder for pour-over?** Not necessarily. A solid hand grinder can deliver excellent grind quality at a lower budget. Buy for the burr setup, adjustment, retention, and workflow you want — not for the price tag alone.

**Is a conical or flat burr better for pour-over?** Neither is universally better. The question is what distribution and flavor profile you want; see [Flat vs Conical Burr Grinders](../burr-technology/flat-vs-conical-burr-grinders.md).

**Can one grinder really do espresso and pour-over?** Yes, if the adjustment range covers both, the burr profile suits you in both, and the workflow tolerates switching. Verify with data and taste rather than the "espresso-capable" label.

**My pour-over is running slow — is the grind too fine?** Maybe, but not necessarily. Agitation, fines migration, paper filters, coffee changes, and pour technique can all contribute. Diagnose before changing the grind.

**Does a motor make better pour-over coffee?** No. A motor solves the cranking and workflow, not grind quality by itself. Convenience is a real upgrade; taste is a separate question.
